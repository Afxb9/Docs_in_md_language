Stage 1 — Weekly Study Plan
Week objective

Primary objective: Move from isolated C-string/pointer understanding into real embedded-style character/serial data handling, while beginning to reconnect C++ with the physical/electronics side of Stage 1.

Secondary objective: Prevent C++ from consuming the entire stage by introducing a small electronics block and a very light code-literacy exercise.

Python: I recommend not introducing a substantial Python block this week. Your current C/C++ dependency is more important, and the Master Curriculum specifically says the supporting tracks should not be treated as equal parallel courses.

Target workload

Normal target: ~4.5–5 hours

Strong week: ~6–7 hours

If life gets busy, the minimum useful version is about 2–3 hours. No catch-up debt.

1. Primary C/C++ topic — Arduino Serial and characters

This should be the main topic of the week.

Your progress record specifically identifies this as the next investigation rather than continuing with more generic C-string functions.

Study experimentally:

char
char[]
char*
std::string

and:

Serial.print()
Serial.println()
Serial.write()

But the important part is not memorizing the APIs.

The goal is to understand the underlying data:

   ↓

   ↓

   ↓
received character/bytes

   ↓

   ↓

Questions you should be able to answer

By the end of this block, you should be able to explain:

What is the difference between 'A' and "A"?
What is the difference between A, 'A', and "A" conceptually?
What does ASCII have to do with serial communication?
What is actually transmitted over Serial?
Why is '\0' different from '\n' and '\r'?
What does Serial.write('A') transmit?
What does Serial.print('A') do?
Why can Serial.write() produce behavior that looks different from Serial.print()?
How does received serial data become a C-string?
Why does strcmp() become useful for command matching?

This directly builds on the competency you just established rather than restarting it.

After the investigation, build something deliberately small.

For example, conceptually:

        ↓

        ↓

This is particularly valuable because your previous Arduino work already involved command characters such as L, C, B, and G. You're now giving the concepts behind that kind of code a proper foundation.

Important competency test

Don't just make it work.

Before running the code, predict:

What is stored in the buffer?
Where is '\0'?
What does strlen() return?
What does strcmp() return for matching commands?
What happens if the command is one character longer than the buffer can hold?
What happens if the terminating '\0' is missing?

That last part is especially important because you've just established buffer overflow and undefined-behavior awareness.

3. Code Literacy — small but deliberate

30–45 minutes only.

This week I would introduce your first explicit unfamiliar-code reading exercise.

Don't immediately read a huge Arduino library.

Instead, take a small Arduino-style function or library snippet and answer:

What data enters this function?
What does it modify?
What does it return?
Where does the buffer come from?
Is it using char, char*, or char[]?
Where does '\0' matter?

Then trace one call:

caller
  ↓
function
  ↓
buffer/pointer
  ↓
comparison
  ↓
return/action

This starts moving you toward the Master Curriculum's Code Literacy target of tracing unfamiliar structured code and eventually following library calls into implementations.

Do not turn this into a separate course. One carefully chosen code-reading exercise is enough this week.

can be meaningful while:

is not the correct C-string content comparison.

without relying on memorized definitions.

The key decision

This is still fundamentally a C/C++ week, but now we're deliberately beginning to make the C++ knowledge embedded, rather than continuing to study language features in isolation.

That's the most appropriate transition point given your current progress.

Your record shows that you have already passed the C-string/buffer competency checkpoint, including pointers, array decay, strlen, strcpy, strcat, strcmp, buffer sizing and undefined behavior.

So I would not spend another week on C strings themselves. The next useful step is applying them to the Arduino/serial context, exactly as your progress record recommends.
